Randomness that makes order

The rule is pure luck: pick a random corner, jump part of the way toward it, drop a dot, repeat. You'd expect a messy cloud. Instead, a perfect fractal appears every single time.

Why does it work?

Each jump is a shrink-toward-a-corner map. With 3 corners and "jump halfway," every map squishes the whole triangle into one of three half-size copies of itself. Copies of copies of copies… that's the Sierpinski triangle.

A set of shrinking maps like this is an iterated function system. The shape it settles onto is called its attractor: no matter where you start, the dots get pulled onto it.

Self-similarity

Zoom into a corner of the triangle and you see the whole triangle again. Shapes made of smaller copies of themselves are fractals. The Sierpinski triangle has dimension log 3 / log 2 ≈ 1.585: more than a line, less than a solid area.

The fern

The fern uses 4 maps instead of corners: one makes the stem, one makes the next smaller leaf, and two make the bottom side leaves. Four rules with six numbers each describe the whole plant.

Every game uses different random picks, yet you get the same picture every time. Mathematicians proved that random picks (almost surely) fill in the exact same shape: the attractor depends only on the rules, not on luck.

Where it shows up

  • Movie and video game landscapes, trees, and clouds
  • Fractal antennas in phones that catch many frequencies
  • Compressing images by finding copies of themselves
