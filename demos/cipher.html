Secret codes are math

Caesar shift = clock arithmetic

Number the letters A = 0, B = 1, …, Z = 25. A Caesar cipher just adds the shift to every letter, wrapping around after Z like hours on a clock:

secret = (letter + shift) mod 26

That's modular arithmetic. To decode, subtract the shift. With only 25 possible shifts, you can just try them all!

Why letter swapping fails

A substitution cipher has 26! ≈ 400,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 possible keys, way too many to try. But it never hides how often each letter appears. E is the most common English letter, then T, A, O, I, N… Match the most common secret letters to those and the code cracks open. This is frequency analysis, invented by the Arab scholar al-Kindi over 1,100 years ago. (It works even better on long messages.)

Vigenère: taking turns

A keyword like CUBE gives four different shifts that take turns, so the same letter gets disguised in different ways. People called it "the unbreakable cipher" for about 300 years, until Charles Babbage and Friedrich Kasiski cracked it in the 1800s by finding the keyword length first.

Today: giant prime numbers

Every time someone buys something online, RSA and similar systems protect the card number. RSA relies on a simple fact: multiplying two huge prime numbers is easy, but going backwards (factoring the answer) is incredibly hard.

A typical RSA key uses a number over 600 digits long. Factoring it with today's best computers and methods would take far longer than the age of the universe. Your online shopping is protected by number theory!

Where it shows up

  • HTTPS (the lock icon), online banking, and chat apps
  • Passwords, digital signatures, and cryptocurrencies
  • Code breakers in World War II helped invent the first computers
