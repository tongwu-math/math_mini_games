Painting with Navier–Stokes

This isn't a video. Your screen is solving the equations of fluid motion about 60 times every second, on a grid of thousands of tiny cells, using the graphics chip.

The equation of motion

∂u/∂t + (u·∇)u = −∇p/ρ + ν∇²u + f

u is the fluid's velocity (which way and how fast it moves at each spot). Each piece of the equation is a rule:

  • ∂u/∂t: how the flow at one spot changes as time ticks.
  • (u·∇)u: the fluid carries itself along. A moving swirl drags its own motion with it.
  • −∇p/ρ: fluid gets pushed from high pressure to low pressure, like air rushing out of a balloon.
  • ν∇²u: stickiness (viscosity). Honey has a big ν, air a tiny one. It smooths out the flow.
  • f: outside pushes. Here, that's your fingers!

No squishing allowed

∇·u = 0

Water can't be squeezed into a smaller space, and it can't appear or vanish. So whatever flows into a cell must flow out. Each frame, the computer solves for the pressure that makes this true (a Poisson equation, ∇²p = ∇·u, solved with 20 rounds of guess-and-improve called Jacobi iteration). Then it subtracts the pressure push from the flow. That's what turns a straight swipe into curling swirls.

How the computer does it

This uses Jos Stam's Stable Fluids method (1999). It traces each cell backwards in time to see where its fluid came from (semi-Lagrangian advection). That trick keeps the simulation from blowing up, even with big time steps. A "swirl boost" (vorticity confinement) brings back small whirls that the coarse grid would otherwise smear out.

Nobody knows whether the 3D Navier–Stokes equations always have smooth solutions, or whether a flow could suddenly "blow up." Proving it either way is one of the Clay Institute's Millennium Prize Problems, worth $1,000,000.

Where it shows up

  • Weather forecasts and climate models
  • Airplane, car, and wind-turbine design
  • Blood flow through hearts and arteries
  • Movie and video-game special effects: smoke, fire, ocean waves
