Speed

Every drawing is secretly circles

Each circle spins at a steady speed: one turns once per loop, the next twice, the next three times, and some spin backwards. Stack them tip-to-tail and the last tip can trace any closed shape you draw.

Spinning arrows

An arrow of length r spinning ω times per loop is the complex number r·eiωt. Your shape is a sum of them:

z(t) = c₀ + c₁eit + c₋₁e−it + c₂e2it + …

Finding the sizes and starting angles ck is the Fourier transform. We sample your drawing at 256 points, solve for 256 circles, and line them up biggest first.

Fewer circles = blurrier

Big, slow circles give the overall outline. Tiny, fast ones add the sharp corners. Throw the tiny ones away and the shape stays recognizable, which is exactly how compression works.

Around 150 AD, astronomers like Ptolemy predicted planet motions using circles riding on circles, called "epicycles." Fourier math explains why that worked so well: with enough circles, you can trace any loop at all.

Where it shows up

  • JPEG photos keep the big waves and toss the tiny ones
  • MP3 music drops the frequencies your ears won't miss
  • MRI scanners measure waves, then a Fourier transform turns them into a picture of your body
  • Noise-cancelling headphones play the opposite wave to erase the noise
