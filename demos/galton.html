Chaos that makes a bell

Every ball is random: at each peg it's a coin flip, left or right. You can't predict where one ball lands. But drop hundreds and the same shape appears every single time.

The binomial distribution

With n rows, a ball's bin number is just how many times it bounced right. The chance of landing in bin k is

P(k) = C(n, k) · pk · (1 − p)n − k

where p is the chance of bouncing right (the "Lean" slider).

Pascal's triangle is hiding in the pegs

Count the different paths from the top to each peg: 1; 1, 1; 1, 2, 1; 1, 3, 3, 1… Each number is the sum of the two above it. That's Pascal's triangle, and those path counts are exactly the C(n, k) in the formula. Middle bins have the most paths, so they get the most balls.

The bell curve and the Central Limit Theorem

As the rows increase, the histogram gets closer and closer to a smooth bell curve (the normal distribution). The Central Limit Theorem says this happens whenever you add up lots of small random things. It's one of the most important ideas in all of math.

With 12 rows there are 2¹² = 4,096 different paths a ball can take. 924 of them end in the middle bin, but only 1 path leads to each edge bin.

Where it shows up

  • Test scores and people's heights are bell-shaped
  • Election polls use it to compute the "margin of error"
  • Factories use it for quality control
  • Medicine trials, insurance, sports stats, weather forecasts
